History

Federal Signal was founded in Chicago by John and James Gilchrist and John Goehst in 1901. It was first called the Federal Electric Company. They began by selling glowing signs for stores. They began making sirens in 1915. They were making almost exclusively sirens by the 50s. The 50s and 60s were eventful for the company, as they became a corporation in '55, changing their name to Federal Sign and Signal Corporation. This would change to simply Federal Signal later on in 1976. But before 1976, in 1961, the company went public. The next year in 1962, they merged with Electrical Products Corporation.

Federal Signal has two groups: Federal Signal Enviornmental Solutions (which is not relevent here), and Federal Signal Safety and Security Systems. The enviornmental side makes cleaning and dumping equipment and vehicles, while the safety and security group makes the sirens.

Their headquarters is currently located in Downers Grove, Illinois.

Model 2

The model 2 has been produced since 1929. It's one of the longest-produced sirens in the world, being produced for over 90 years. Originally called the FedelCode Type Mach 2, it was later simplified to the model 2

It's only 2-horsepower and has a sound output of 100-102 dB at 100 feet. This siren requires only moderate power and is the cheapest option. It's quite small and compact, too. It was and still is quite a simple siren. There are many slightly different variations of this siren. It's usually mounted on buildings or mounted on long "legs" on roofs of buildings. It's rarer to find them mounted on poles. These were intended to be on fire stations, so they are mostly found in red or white, though other colors do exist.

Federal Signal has other sirens that look like this siren, such as the Model 3, 5, and 7.

2001

Arguably their most popular siren is the 2001. It is very common to find in the suburbs and decently newer areas. They are rotational sirens and mounted on poles. They were made as a replacement for the Thunderbolt tornado sirens, and generally aging sirens. Because of this, many old sirens were replaced with these, causing them to spread everywhere.

These sirens have been made for more then 30 years at this point, as they began being manufactured in 1988. It, along with the 508, are Federal Signal's most popular sirens. There are multiple different kinds of 2001 sirens, all looking pretty much the same. the key differences are the first generation had a rounded top to their back, and the 2001-130 and Equinox (not officially part of the general 2001 group but taking the same apprearance aand shape) have the federal signal logo in the middle. The Equinox, however, is more lower pitched than the other 2001 sirens. They, as a group range from 6 to 6.5 horsepower and are about 125 to 130 dB at 100 feet. According to the Federal Signal website, the 2001-130 Equinox can be ordered to run on AC or solar power with a battery backup

These sirens are most commonly white, buut they can also be yellow. There is a custmo painted green one by the Girl Scouts of America in Indiana.

This is a Federal Signal 2001-130 Equinox taken from the Federal signal website. Notice how the top is flat, not rounded? Almost all 2001 sirens are not of the roundback variant, as those were the first made.

500

A lesser-known siren by Federal Signal is the 500. It is a rotational siren, much like the 2001 in appearance, resembling the 2001 Roundback variant. However, they are not the same. They were intended to be more middle range in distance compared to other sirens. They were intended to be mounted on buildings, as they had a flat bottom. However, many cities opted to mount them on poles, which required extra supports, as they were not intented to be pole-mounts.

When they were first made, they very much did not reseble the 2001, or rather, the 2001 resemble them. The 500 was made in 1953, many years before the 2001. It was not until the third generation, in 1970, that they had a redesign, pictured below.

As you can see, this redesigned version looks like the upcoming roundback 2001. However, there are some differences. Most notably, the hole in the center. 2001 sirens did not have a hole in the middle of their center cone, or nose cone. 500 sirens did.

The Federal Signal 500 failed for quite a few reasons:

  • Bad advertising and bad sales
  • On the more expensive side
  • Lack of replacement parts
  • The lower belt could not be replaced unless you dismantled the whole siren
  • Failing or broken rotators due to poor design
  • Early replacement

This siren ranged from 120 to 130 dB at 100 feet, and was 10 horsepower, 15 to 20 fir the 500-DHTT.

508

Not to be confused with the 500, the 508 is a newer siren which began production in 2011. Its full name is the 508-128, but it is commonly referred to as the 508. Federal Signal really likes their numbers, I suppose. Anyway, they were originally made to be used near powerplants. Of course, they are one of the most popular Federal Signal sirens along with the 2001.

Because of the nose cone being an unusual shape, it also is reffered to as the cereal bowl, as the cone resembles a bowl. While it may look like the 2001 because it is most commonly found in white, has a round horn, is rotational, and other physical features, it stands out as its own siren for a multitude of reasons:

  • Its back is more broad and its sides are shorter, causing the whole siren to be more of a short, snub-nosed looking siren
  • It has a hole in the nose cone, unlike the 2001. Its nose cone is also more concave.
  • The back has indents that, together, resemble a sort of flower shape. The 2001 lacks such indents.

According to the Federal Signal website, the 508 can be AC powered or solar powered with a battery backup. This siren is can produce 128 dB at 100 feet, which is why the siren is called the 508-128.

DSA

The Federal Signal Directional Speaker Array, or DSA, is a siren tht is directional, as the name states. This means it does not rotate and instead emits sounds to one specific direction - the direction it is facing. Because of this, they need to be placed in groups that face multiple different directions to properly cover the radius. This siren was first made in 1990 and continues to be made to this day. It has vertical groupings of two to six sirens, holding up to four horiontally mounted.

The DSA produces 111 to 121 dB at 100 feet and, because it is electric, has a wattage of 200-600. The decibels this siren can produce depends on how many speakers there are per array. As stated before, there are two to six per array (vertical). Just two per array can produce 111, while four produces 117, and six produces 121. There can also be odd numbers of speakers.

These sirens are named by the number of speakers in each array and the number of arrays (in that order).

This, from the Federal Signal website, is a DSA 4x2, as it has 2 arrays with 4 speakers on each.

According to the Federal Signal website, each speaker has a 70 degree horizontal angle of dispersion. Depending on how many arrays there are, 180 degree coverage is possible. You can also change the downward tilt of the speaker arrays.

Eclipse

The Eclipse was first made in 2005, so it is a decently new siren. This siren can produce 107-115 dB at 100 feet and has 6.5 horsepower. The Eclipse was made to give Federal Signal a DC siren, as demand for DC powered sirens was increasing during that time. The main model of the siren is the Eclipse 8, but the other one is the Eclipse NH, which is the less popular of the two. Both will be covered in this section.

Because the Eclipse 8 is the more popular one, it is the one Federal Signal has on their website. It is omnidirectional, so it covers 360 degrees. It is silver and can be pole-mounted or on roofs. Some are even mounted on water towers. I know this because I have seen one in person mounted on a water tower!

This is the Eclipse 8.

The Eclipse NH is not as popular. The NH stands for "No horns" because it has no horns. That is done to make it cheaper, although it reduces its performance. It is special order only. Because it has no horns, it can only do 107 dB at 100 feet. Becayse it is not as good, removed from public catalogs (being special order) and having less advertising than the Eclipse 8, it became the lesser of the two sirens in the Eclipse lineup.

EOWS

The EOWS, or Electronic Outdoor Warning Siren, were made from 1981 to 2007. It can be either omnidirectional or rotational depending on the model. This siren is also known as the SiraTone.

There are many kinds of EOWS, such as the 115, 408, 612, 812, and 1212. Federal Signal seems to really like their numbers.

This is an EOWS 115. It is omnidirectional.

This here is an EOWS 812. It is rotational.

This series was Federal Signal's first attempt at making electronic sirens. They produce 113 - 124 dB at 100 feet, with a wattage of 800 to 1200 watts. Nowadays, quite a few have been replaced by newer sirens.

Modulator

The Modulator was produced from 1990 to 2013. Its successor, the Modulator II is still currently made today since 2013. This siren is Federal Signal's best-selling siren. They produce a range of 106 - 125 dB at 100 feet. It was made to replace the EOWS 1212.

The siren is set up in this way: siren cells are placed on top of eachother, each with a driver, except the bottom cell. The bottom cell is there to evenly disperse the sound of the above cells. What makes this siren so unique, in fact, is that they are cells rather than speakers. There can be up to 7 cells per siren. These sirens are named by the amount of cells (except the bottom one) there are, and then the number of drivers there are. For example, a siren which has three cells excluding the bottom dispersion cell and has 24 drivers would be called the MOD3024 (MOD for Modulator). Simple enough! And again, Federal Signal likes their numbers! For the Modulator II, there is a B tacked on to the end of the name. There is no 3024 for B, but there is a 3012B.

SD-10

The SD-10 stands for Special Dual-Tone, 10-horsepower. This omnidirectional siren was produced from 1954 to 1987. They were made for civil defense and the cold war. They are 109 dB at 100 feet and have a horsepower of 7.5 to 10.

It was made as a dual-tone siren because at the time, the Office of Civil Defense required all sirens for civil defense purposes had to be dual-tone.

There was also a rotational variant called the 500-SHTT.

This is an SD-10. They mostly are seen in yellow. As you can see, this one still has the Civil Defense logo on the side. That shows how old it is!

STH-10 and STL-10

This siren had two variants, being the STH-10A and the STH-10B. The name stands for Single Tone High, 10 horsepower. The A variant was made from 1955 to 2005, and the B variant from 1955 to 1995. This siren is omnidirectional. It is one of Federal Signal's most popular omnidirectional sirens. This siren was made to mostly be used at fire stations. It was also used for civil defense and weather, but primarily fires.

The STH-10 had a reputation for being easy to maintain. It was quite reliable. So reliable, in fact, that even after being discontinued, they can still be commonly found, still operational in most cases. It is one of the most commonly found sirens in the arctic region. That is how you know it is durable!

The STH-10A and STH-10B had some differences:

  • The A variant used a 10 horsepower motor while the B variant used a 7.5 horsepower motor.
  • Both got discontinued in 1995, but the A version got reintroduced, while the B did not.
  • the A version was three-phase. The B was only one.

The STH-10 sirens made after their reintroduction were of a lesser quality. When they were finally discontinued, they were replaced with the Eclipse 8. A Brazilian siren manufacturer by the name of Engesig still manufactured the STH-10 under the name EG-109 TITAN. There are more variants, than just that, though. They are lesser-known. W.S Darley & Co. made their own version called the Type 10. It would be made until the 1960s. There was also the STH-10C which was made for an unknown period of time. It's official name is also unknown, but people call it the STH-10C. It most likely was a siren you could get for special order, so few exist.

Left is the Darley model. Notice how it has its own distinct look dispite being essentially an inverted STH-10. On the right it the only known picture of an STH-C.

The STH-10 was most commonly found in white, yellow, or red. Other colors exist, such as a siren with alternating black and white horns. They can be mounted on poles or roofs.

This siren was made as a higher pitched version of the STL-10. The STL-10 standed for Single Tone Low, 10 horsepower. It was also omnidirectional. The STH series ended up outselling the STL by quite a bit, and thus, harder to find.

Here is a photo of the STL-10. Notice how it has fewer horns than the STH.

This siren was made from 1953 to 1995 and was 114 dB at 100 feet. It was also made for fire stations, but it was good at blocking out traffic sounds in urban areas because of its low tone.

This siren has also been found to have square horns sometimes. That is rare, though.

Thunderbeam

Not to be confused with the Thunderbolt, the Thunderbeam (also known as the model RSH-10) was made to replace the 500-SH. The RSH-10 is for Rotating Single-tone High, 10 horsepower. It was made from 1983 to 1995. It had a range of 122 - 124 dB at 100 feet and was 10 horsepower, as the name states. The goal of this siren was to amplify the sound of an omnidirectional siren without horns or a rotator assembly. With that, it is classed as a hybrid siren.

This siren wasn't perfect, though. There was a possibility that, while running, the gearbox could strip the gears and make the disc spin super fast, which could damage the siren even more.

Everything was in a wire mesh cage. Sound was directed downwards while preventing any damage to the siren. At first, this was quite expirimental. They tried adding horns to it, but later scrapped it. None of that type, the RSL-10, exist today. However, the RSH-10 still remains. It was used at fire stations, for civil defense, and weather, of course.

Thunderbolt

This siren is one of the most well-known sirens. The Thunderbolt remains iconic, even though it was only made from 1952 to 1990. It was rotational and produced 125 - 130 dB of sound at 100 feet, which was impressive. It was only 2 horsepower, too. Its blower was 7.5 horsepower, though, so a combined 9.5. From 1952 to 1954, most of these sirnes had steel reinforcements in the horn. In 1954, there was a dual tone variant made, called the Thunderbolt1000T. It became the standard Thunderbolt later on because the Office of Civil Defense required all sirens used for civil defense had to be dual tone.

This iconic line came to an end when the 2001 was introduced, outperforming it without the need for a blower and without a battery backup.

Thunderbolts came in large ranges of colors, from white to yellow to black and yellow stripes. Some were even red.

Many of these have been replaced with newer sirens as they sadly have wasted away. Some remain, operating to this day, but many are gone. They stand as an iconic relic of Federal Signal, the cold war period, civil defense, and tornado sirens as a whole.

2T22 and 3T22

The 2T22 and 3T22 are omnidirectional sirens made from 1955 to 1992. These sirens were named because the 2T22 has two tones and has 22 horns/ports, while the 3T22 has three tones. This siren produces 113 dB at 100 feet. They are either 7.5 or 10 horsepower. People refer to both sirens together as XT22, but that is not a name used by Federal Signal themselves.

The 3T22 was designed for civil defense and fire stations. The 2T22 would be released after the 3T22 as a cheaper alternative. Both were quite popular, but later discontinued to make way for the STH-10. 2T22 and 3T22 sirens are reliable, and many stand to this day. Some have been replaced, but they are mostly still operational. Because the sirens' horns were made of thin sheet metal, many can be found with some sort of damage such as warping or denting. Some sirens are even missing horns because of this. What is interesting abou the 3T22, though, is that even though it had three signals, most places didn't use the third signal. This third signal was the pulse, or hi-lo signal. Because the 2T22 lacks the ability to make the hi-lo signal, it requires less mechanisms, and is cheaper to make. It requires far less maintenance.

These sirens usually are red or yellow, and sometimes even white.
